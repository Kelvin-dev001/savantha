Most family budgets fail for the same reason: they are lists of amounts, drawn up in a good month. A budget that holds is an order of priorities. It decides what gets paid first, what waits, and what gives way when money is short. Build the order once, agree it as a family, and every month after that is a shorter conversation.

This guide sets out a five-line framework we use with households, and the few habits that keep it standing through a real Kenyan year.

## Why do most budgets fall apart?

Because real life is lumpy and a list of amounts is not. A typical household year has January school fees, a December journey home, a car that needs tyres, a relative in hospital, a harambee you did not plan for. Many incomes are lumpy too: business takings, commission, a harvest, a contract that pays late.

A budget written as "this much on food, this much on transport" has no answer when one of those arrives. It simply breaks, and the family goes back to managing money from memory. The fix is not a more detailed list. It is a clear order.

## What are the five lines, and in what order?

Every shilling that comes in is given a place, in this order. The order is about priority: it tells you what is protected first and what gives way first.

| Line           | What goes in it                                                            | The rule that keeps it honest                  |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Essentials  | Rent, food, transport, utilities, health cover, school fees spread monthly | Costed from real statements, not memory        |
| 2. Reserve     | An emergency fund, kept apart from everyday money                          | Moved on payday, before anything else is spent |
| 3. Commitments | Support for family, chama contributions, giving                            | A fixed amount, agreed in advance              |
| 4. Debt        | Mobile loans, credit, hire purchase, salary advances                       | The most expensive is cleared first            |
| 5. Goals       | Land, a house deposit, education, a business, retirement                   | One goal per pot, with an amount and a date    |

What remains after the five lines is yours to spend freely, without guilt. Spending money is part of the plan, not a failure of it.

### Line 1: essentials

The costs that keep the household running. Take them from your M-Pesa and bank statements for the last three months, not from memory; most families underestimate food and transport. Include anything that is really a monthly cost even if it is not paid monthly, such as school fees (more on that below).

### Line 2: the reserve

Money set aside for the bad month, so that an emergency does not become a debt. Start with a target of one month of essentials. Once you reach it, build towards three to six months. Keep it in a separate account or wallet that is easy to reach in an emergency and slightly inconvenient the rest of the time.

### Line 3: commitments to others

In many Kenyan families this line is the one that quietly breaks the budget, because it is decided request by request. Decide it once instead: a set amount each month for support to parents and relatives, your chama, and your giving. Agree it with your spouse. When a request comes, the question becomes "does it fit this month's commitment?", which is easier to answer, and kinder, than a yes or no on the spot.

### Line 4: debt

List every debt with what it costs you to carry. Keep up the minimum on all of them, and put anything extra towards the most expensive first; short-term mobile loans are usually at the top. While you are clearing debt, stop borrowing for everyday spending, or the line never empties.

### Line 5: goals

The reason for everything above. Give each goal its own pot, an amount and a date: "a plot deposit by December 2028" is a plan; "save for land" is a wish. Fund one or two goals well rather than five goals a little.

## Why plan from your lowest month?

Because an average month does not pay the rent in a bad one. If your income varies, look back over the past year, find your lowest normal month (ignore a true one-off), and build the five lines on that figure.

In a better month, the extra follows the same order: top up the reserve first, then clear expensive debt, then add to goals. Good months fund the bad ones, instead of raising your spending to a level only good months can pay for.

## How do you handle school fees and other lumpy costs?

Turn them into monthly lines. Add up everything the year will ask for: fees for all three terms, uniforms, books, trips, the insurance premium, the December journey, the car service. Divide by twelve. Move that amount every month into a separate place, and pay the bills from there when they arrive.

January then stops being a crisis. The money is already waiting, and the reserve stays untouched for real emergencies.

## How do you make the plan run itself?

Willpower runs out by the third week of the month. Structure does not. A few arrangements do most of the work:

- **Separate places for separate jobs.** One account or wallet for essentials, one for the reserve, one for lumpy costs, one for each goal. Money that sits together gets spent together.
- **Payday transfers.** Move the reserve, the lumpy-cost amount and your commitments on the day the money arrives, with standing orders where you can.
- **One person holds the record.** Not to control the money, but so that someone always knows where it stands.

## What do you do when a bad month comes?

You cut from the bottom of the order upwards, in a sequence decided in advance, not in a panic:

1. Reduce free spending first.
2. Pause contributions to goals. Pausing is not failing; restart them as soon as you can.
3. Talk to family early if this month's commitments must be smaller. Early is kinder than late.
4. Keep paying at least the minimum on every debt.
5. Use the reserve for essentials. That is exactly what it is for.

Then rebuild in the same order: the reserve first, before goals restart. And avoid the most expensive move of all, which is borrowing at high cost to keep up with commitments.

<Note label="The monthly review, thirty minutes" tone="key">

Once a month, sit down together and go through five things:

1. What came in, and what went out, line by line.
2. The reserve: where it stands against its target.
3. Debts: what was cleared, and what is next.
4. The month ahead: any lumpy cost, request or change coming.
5. One decision to take forward. Only one.

</Note>

## Where do you start?

This week, do three things. Pull three months of statements and cost your essentials honestly. Open a separate place for the reserve and move something into it, however small. And agree a number for commitments with your spouse.

The framework will not make a hard month easy. What it does is make every month predictable: you will know, before it happens, what is protected and what gives way. That is what a budget that holds feels like.
