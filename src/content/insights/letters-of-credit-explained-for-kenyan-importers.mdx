A letter of credit is a written undertaking by your bank to pay your supplier, on your behalf, once the supplier presents documents that match the credit's terms exactly. It lets two businesses that do not yet trust each other trade safely. Your supplier knows a bank will pay. You know payment will only be made against proof that the goods were shipped as agreed.

That is the idea. In practice, a credit is only as good as its terms, and most of the trouble importers run into comes from terms that were agreed in a hurry. This guide walks through who is involved, how a credit runs from start to finish, what it costs, and how to keep it from going wrong.

## Who is involved in a letter of credit?

At least four parties, often five. Most credits worldwide follow the same international rules, the ICC's Uniform Customs and Practice for Documentary Credits, known as UCP 600, so the roles are the same whether your supplier is in Guangzhou, Dubai or Mumbai.

| Party           | Usually                           | What they do                                                                       |
| --------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Applicant       | You, the importer                 | Ask your bank to issue the credit, and reimburse it when it pays                   |
| Beneficiary     | Your supplier                     | Ships the goods and presents the documents to be paid                              |
| Issuing bank    | Your bank in Kenya                | Issues the credit and undertakes to pay against compliant documents                |
| Advising bank   | A bank in the supplier's country  | Checks the credit is genuine and passes it to the supplier                         |
| Confirming bank | Optional, often the advising bank | Adds its own undertaking to pay, so the supplier is not relying on your bank alone |

## How does a letter of credit work, step by step?

From agreeing the deal to collecting the goods, a typical import credit runs in eight steps.

1. **You agree the terms with your supplier.** The sales contract says payment is by letter of credit, and fixes the Incoterm (such as FOB or CIF), the documents required, the latest shipment date and the expiry date.
2. **You apply to your bank.** The bank assesses your credit, then sets a limit on a trade facility or asks you to hold a cash margin against the credit.
3. **Your bank issues the credit.** It sends the credit, by SWIFT, to the advising bank in your supplier's country.
4. **Your supplier checks the terms.** If anything cannot be met, this is the moment to ask for an amendment, before goods are shipped.
5. **Your supplier ships and presents documents.** The bill of lading, invoice and other required documents go to the bank named in the credit.
6. **The banks examine the documents.** Under UCP 600, a bank has up to five banking days after presentation to decide whether they comply.
7. **Payment is made.** If the documents comply, the supplier is paid at sight or on the agreed later date, and your bank debits you or your facility.
8. **You receive the documents and clear the goods.** Your bank releases the documents you need to clear the shipment, usually through the Port of Mombasa.

The order matters. Every important choice is made in steps 1 and 4, before anything ships. Once the documents are presented, the banks look only at the paper.

<Note label="The one rule to remember" tone="key">

Banks deal in documents, not in goods. If the documents match the credit, your bank must pay, even if the goods turn out to be wrong (outside rare cases of fraud). If they do not match, it need not pay, even if the goods are perfect. Everything you need to be sure of has to be written into the documents the credit asks for.

</Note>

## Which documents should the credit ask for?

Ask for the documents that prove what you actually need to be true. A typical import credit asks for:

- **A commercial invoice,** describing the goods exactly as the credit does.
- **A transport document:** a clean, on-board bill of lading for sea freight, or an air waybill.
- **A packing list,** so you and customs can check the shipment.
- **A certificate of origin.** It can affect the duty you pay, for example under EAC or COMESA preferences.
- **An insurance certificate,** if the Incoterm makes your supplier responsible for cover (as CIF and CIP do).
- **An independent inspection certificate,** if quality or quantity matters to you. This is how you bring the goods themselves into a document-based process.
- **A Certificate of Conformity,** if the goods are regulated under the Kenya Bureau of Standards' Pre-Export Verification of Conformity (PVoC) programme.

Keep the list to what you need. Every extra document is another place for a discrepancy.

## What will your bank ask for?

Issuing a credit is a form of lending: your bank promises to pay before it has your money. Expect it to assess your business much as it would for a loan. It will usually want:

- Your company documents, and know-your-customer details for directors and signatories.
- Recent financial statements and bank statements, to show the business can carry the cost.
- A trade facility limit, security, or a cash margin set aside against the credit.
- The proforma invoice or sales contract, so it can see what it is undertaking to pay for.
- Your import documentation. Many banks ask for the Import Declaration Form (IDF) reference, lodged through KenTrade's single window, before they issue.

A well-prepared file, with these in order, is often the difference between a credit issued in days and one that waits for weeks.

## What does a letter of credit cost?

Credits are not free, and the charges are spread across several lines, so ask for every one of them in writing before you apply. Typical charges include:

- An issuing commission, usually linked to the amount and how long the credit runs.
- Amendment fees, each time the terms change.
- Advising and confirmation fees, which the contract should say whether you or your supplier pays.
- Discrepancy fees, charged when documents do not match.
- SWIFT, courier and handling charges.

There is also a cost that never appears on a tariff: the facility or cash margin tied up while the credit is open. Count it when you compare a credit with other ways of paying.

## How does it compare with other ways of paying?

A letter of credit sits between the two extremes. Paying in advance puts all the risk on you; open account (paying after delivery) puts it all on your supplier. The right choice depends on how well you know each other, and on what each side can afford to carry.

| Method                 | How it works                                      | Risk to you | Risk to your supplier |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------- | --------------------- |
| Advance payment        | You pay before shipment                           | High        | Low                   |
| Letter of credit       | A bank pays against compliant documents           | Lower       | Lower                 |
| Documentary collection | Banks pass documents, but do not undertake to pay | Moderate    | Moderate              |
| Open account           | You pay after delivery                            | Low         | High                  |

## Why do letters of credit go wrong?

Mostly because the documents do not match the credit. Banks examine them strictly, and a small mismatch is enough to refuse. The usual causes are:

- **Late shipment,** after the latest date the credit allows.
- **Late presentation.** Under UCP 600, original transport documents must be presented within 21 days of shipment unless the credit says otherwise, and always before the credit expires.
- **Inconsistent details,** such as a description, quantity or port that differs between documents.
- **Missing or wrong documents,** signatures or endorsements.

A discrepancy does not have to end the deal. Your bank will usually ask whether you accept the documents anyway, and you can choose to waive it. But it costs time and fees, and it hands the decision to you at the worst moment, with the goods already on the water.

## What should you settle before you apply?

Most of the work happens before the credit exists. Agree these with your supplier, in writing, first.

<Note label="Settle before you apply" tone="key">

1. The Incoterm, and therefore who arranges freight and cover.
2. A latest shipment date and an expiry date your supplier can realistically meet.
3. A short list of documents, each one tied to something you need to be true.
4. Whether the credit should be confirmed, and who pays for confirmation.
5. Who pays which bank charges, on your side and theirs.
6. Payment at sight or at a later date, and how that fits your cash flow.

</Note>

A credit built on clear terms is quiet: the supplier ships, the documents match, the banks pay, and you collect your goods. That is what you are aiming for.
