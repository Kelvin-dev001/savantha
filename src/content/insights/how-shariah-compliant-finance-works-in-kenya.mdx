Shariah-compliant finance is finance arranged so that no one earns money simply by lending money. Instead of charging interest, the financier buys, leases or shares in something real, and earns from that trade or partnership. In Kenya you can use it for a home, a car, savings, cover and business finance. It is open to anyone, whatever their faith.

This guide explains how it works in practice, the structures you are most likely to be offered, and how to tell a well-structured product from one that only carries the name.

## What makes finance Shariah-compliant?

A product is Shariah-compliant when its structure, not only its name, passes five tests. These are the same principles we use when we screen an option for a client.

- **No riba (interest).** Money may not earn money by itself. Any profit must come from trade, a lease or a shared venture, never from a fixed charge on a loan.
- **No gharar (excessive uncertainty).** Both sides must know what is being exchanged, at what price and on what terms. Vague contracts and hidden conditions fail.
- **No maysir (speculation).** No gambling, and no arrangement where one side's gain depends on chance rather than effort, trade or a real asset.
- **Asset-backed.** Every transaction is tied to something you can point to: a house, stock, equipment, a business activity.
- **Ethical sectors.** No alcohol, gambling, tobacco, conventional interest-based finance or other prohibited activity, directly or through a partner.

The practical consequence is simple. In a Shariah-compliant deal, the financier takes on some of the responsibilities and risks of owning the asset for a time. That is what makes the profit legitimate, and it is also what you should look for in the contract.

## Who offers it in Kenya, and who regulates it?

Kenya has full Islamic banks, and several conventional banks run Islamic "windows": separate Shariah-compliant products and accounts inside a conventional bank. Beyond banking, there are takaful operators (Shariah-compliant cover), and some unit trust, pension and savings providers offer Shariah-compliant options.

There is no separate regulator for Islamic finance. A Shariah-compliant product is regulated by the same body as its conventional equivalent:

| Product                                         | Regulator                            |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Bank accounts and home, car or business finance | Central Bank of Kenya                |
| Takaful (cover)                                 | Insurance Regulatory Authority       |
| Unit trusts, funds and sukuk                    | Capital Markets Authority            |
| Pension schemes                                 | Retirement Benefits Authority        |
| Savings and credit co-operatives                | SACCO Societies Regulatory Authority |

Shariah approval is a separate question. Each provider appoints its own Shariah supervisory board or adviser, who reviews and approves its products. That approval tells you the product was checked. It does not replace your own understanding of what you are signing.

## Which structures are you likely to meet?

Most Shariah-compliant products in Kenya are built from a handful of contracts. Knowing which one you are being offered tells you who owns what, when, and who carries which risk.

### Murabaha: a sale at a disclosed mark-up

The financier buys the asset (a car, stock, equipment, sometimes a home) and sells it to you at its cost plus a mark-up that is disclosed and agreed up front. You pay the price in instalments. Once agreed, the sale price is fixed: it does not rise if market rates move.

**Check:** that the financier genuinely owns the asset before selling it to you, and what happens to the price if you settle early.

### Ijara: a lease, often ending in ownership

The financier buys the asset and leases it to you for an agreed rent. In "lease to own" versions, ownership passes to you at the end, by a separate sale or gift. Because the financier owns the asset during the lease, the major costs of ownership, such as structural repairs and insuring the asset itself, usually sit with the financier.

**Check:** how and when the rent can be reviewed, and which costs the contract passes to you.

### Diminishing musharaka: a shared home you buy out

You and the financier buy the property together. You live in it, pay rent on the financier's share, and buy that share from them piece by piece. As your share grows, your rent falls, until the home is wholly yours.

**Check:** how the price of each share is set, how the rent is reviewed, and what happens to both shares if the property has to be sold.

### Mudaraba: profit-sharing savings

Many Islamic savings and term accounts work on profit sharing. Your money is put to work in Shariah-compliant activity, and any profit is shared in a ratio agreed in advance. Some accounts use wakala (agency) instead: the provider puts your money to work on your behalf for a disclosed fee. Either way, the profit is not fixed or promised, and in principle the money placed is at risk.

**Check:** which contract the account uses, how the profit ratio or fee is set, how often profit is paid, and how past distributions were calculated.

### Takaful: cover as mutual help

Members contribute to a shared fund that pays claims. The operator manages the fund for a fee, and any surplus may be shared with members. Takaful is available for health, motor, property and family (life) cover.

**Check:** how the operator is paid, how surplus is treated, and the exclusions, as you would with any cover.

### Sukuk: certificates of ownership

Sukuk are certificates that represent ownership in an asset or project, and pay holders from the income it produces. They matter more to institutions and pension funds than to most households, and Kenya's market for them is still young.

## Is it cheaper or more expensive than conventional finance?

Neither, as a rule. The total cost of a Shariah-compliant home or car finance can be close to the conventional equivalent, and pricing is often set with reference to conventional rates. That is not in itself a sign that the product is non-compliant, but it does mean you should compare them on the same terms.

Compare the total amount you will pay over the life of the contract, in shillings, not just the monthly figure. Then compare the parts that differ: who owns the asset and when, who pays for repairs and cover, the charges for paying late, and what happens if you settle early or cannot continue.

<Note label="A fair comparison">

Line up each option on the same five rows: total cost, ownership at each stage, costs passed to you, late-payment terms, and early settlement. If a provider cannot answer one of the five in writing, that is your answer.

</Note>

## How is compliance checked?

Compliance is checked twice. First by the provider's own Shariah board or adviser, who approves the product before it is offered. Second, and just as important, by reading the actual contract: a product can be approved in principle and still be documented badly.

When we screen an option for a client, we read the structure, test it against the five principles, and note anything that needs a closer look. We do not issue fatwas or Shariah rulings. If you want a personal ruling on a specific contract, we encourage you to take it to your own scholar, and we can prepare a short note to make that conversation easier. Our Shariah reviewer oversees this screening work.

## What should you ask before you commit?

Good providers welcome these questions. Ask them, and ask for the answers in writing.

<Note label="Ask before you commit" tone="key">

1. Which contract is this (murabaha, ijara, musharaka or another), and who approved it as Shariah-compliant?
2. Who owns the asset at each stage, and when does ownership pass to me?
3. What will I pay in total over the life of the contract, in shillings?
4. Which costs of ownership, such as repairs, cover and fees, fall on me?
5. What happens if I pay late? Where do any late-payment charges go?
6. Can I settle early, and is any rebate written into the contract or left to the provider's discretion?
7. What happens to the asset, and to what I have paid, if I cannot continue?

</Note>

The answers will tell you more than the product's name. They also tell you whether the structure suits your goals and your appetite for risk, which is the question that matters most: a compliant product can still be the wrong one for you.
