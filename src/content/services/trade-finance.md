faqs:
  - q: What is a letter of credit, and when do I need one?
    a: >-
      A letter of credit is a bank's undertaking to pay the seller once they present documents
      that match agreed terms. It suits new trading relationships, large orders and suppliers
      who will not ship on open account. It costs more than simpler methods, so we compare it
      with the alternatives first.
  - q: What is the difference between a letter of credit and a documentary collection?
    a: >-
      With a documentary collection, banks pass the shipping documents to the buyer against
      payment or acceptance, but neither bank undertakes to pay. It is cheaper and simpler, and
      leaves more risk with the seller. A letter of credit adds a bank's undertaking, at a
      higher cost. Trust between the parties usually decides.
  - q: Can you get my business a trade finance facility?
    a: >-
      We do not lend or arrange credit on a bank's behalf. We prepare you to apply: we help you
      choose the right structure, assemble the figures and documents the bank will ask for, and
      rehearse the questions it is likely to raise. That preparation often shapes the terms you
      are offered.
  - q: How do you help with currency risk on imports?
    a: >-
      We show you how much a move in the shilling against the dollar, euro or yuan would change
      the landed cost of your goods, and the ways businesses usually manage that exposure. We do
      not trade currency or recommend specific contracts; we make sure you understand the risk
      before you agree a price.
  - q: Why do letters of credit get rejected for discrepancies?
    a: >-
      Banks pay against documents, not goods, so a misspelt name, a late shipment date or a
      missing certificate can stop payment. Most discrepancies are avoidable. We check the
      credit terms against what your supplier can actually present, and review the documents
      before they reach the bank.
  - q: Do you only work with importers?
    a: >-
      No. We work with importers, exporters and companies that do both. For exporters, the
      question is usually how to make sure you are paid for what you ship, and how quickly. We
      look at the same options from the other side of the deal, with the same attention to cost
      and risk.
